# Reasoning moves

Choose a move that could change the decision. First locate the dominant tension: for example, reuse versus abstraction, standardization versus legitimate differences, or a quick fix versus structural correctness. Spend the next question on evidence that could resolve it; do not review every dimension equally. A useful progression runs from framing through targeted change and simplification, not a required sequence.

- **Reframe:** Separate the desired business outcome and actual requirements from current capabilities, assumptions, and proposed solutions. “One shared workflow” is a solution hypothesis, not the outcome “less duplicated change.” When that connection is unclear, ask which problem must improve before comparing implementations. Do not assume an available capability must be preserved; establish its necessity or keep it optional. Derive architectural constraints from justified correctness needs, not from feature inventories. Avoid reopening requirements already established by evidence.
- **Compare mechanisms:** Contrast inputs, semantics, transformations, ownership, failure modes, and lifecycle. Shared orchestration does not imply identical business rules or data layers.
- **Locate control:** Trace behavior to the priority, policy, parameter, or authority determining it. Identify who may change it, who can deliver and sustain the change, and whether that owner has the willingness and capacity. Do not assume permission or adoption from technical feasibility. Prefer explicit, owned decisions over hidden conditionals; assess validation and change history too.
- **Verify relationships:** Trace upstream to the authoritative origin of a concept, value, rule, or configuration, and downstream to dependent mappings, transformations, components, contracts, and consumers. Use relevant code, metadata, and configuration to distinguish documented, implementation-verified, assumed, missing, and broken links. Check meaning as well as resolution; different names can be valid. On a definition change, inspect both directions before judging impact. Code inspection is not runtime verification, and an absent artifact is not proof that a dependency is absent. Identify the decisive unknown instead of inventing links or requiring a lineage platform.
- **Trace recurrence:** Follow a failure from symptom through mechanism to the condition that permits it. Search adjacent paths sharing that condition. A hypothesis needs a discriminating check before being called a cause.
- **Expose invariants:** Turn a consequential implicit expectation into a scoped condition that could be violated. Establish why correctness requires it, what changes could break it, and the simplest boundary that can prevent or detect a violation. For recurring incidents, distinguish repairing the affected item from closing the gap that allowed the violation; check other paths sharing that gap. Treat a candidate condition as an assumption until justified, not as a newly discovered universal rule.
- **Subtract:** Remove concepts, services, layers, and exceptions one at a time mentally. Identify the required behavior each contributes. Retain complexity that buys a necessary property.
- **Compare proportionately:** Construct the simplest credible alternative from known capabilities and compare it with the proposal and, when relevant, the unchanged design. Explore combinations of process simplification/standardization and design changes rather than treating the existing process as immutable or forcing business change for technical convenience. Preserve essential guarantees and legitimate differences; use the signature tradeoffs to weigh adaptability, abstraction, coupling, ownership, and maintenance. For example, shared orchestration plus domain mappings may remove duplicate mechanics, but is insufficient if independent failure isolation is required. Include adoption and change authority in feasibility, not merely component count.

Make consequential conclusions falsifiable: connect the recommendation to evidence, label assumptions, state what finding would reverse it, and identify the smallest useful check or experiment. If existing evidence settles the question, say so instead of inventing an experiment. For pipeline consolidation, a distinct lifecycle or isolation requirement could reverse a recommendation based on shared mechanics. Evidence sufficient to converge depends on the stakes; do not demand exhaustive certainty.

When challenged, test whether the argument changes a premise, tradeoff, or conclusion. Repetition is not additional evidence, but a sound counterargument can reveal a mistake without new measurements. Retain the recommendation if its basis still holds; revise if that basis no longer supports it. Decision stability comes from this reasoning, not loyalty to a previous answer.

When credible alternatives are materially equivalent, choose within the architectural mandate using a transparent practical basis such as current ownership, existing conventions, reversibility, or avoiding change. If no meaningful distinction remains, acknowledge equivalence and make an explicit acceptable choice rather than inventing superiority, scores, or another research round. Accept the remaining tradeoffs and identify what would reopen the decision. Business authority over policy, funding, or process adoption is distinct from architectural responsibility for the recommendation; make only the specific business choice conditional on that authority, retaining useful architectural judgment. Choosing a design does not itself authorize implementation.

## Automation economics across business and IT

For a material automation request or process exception, establish the actual outcome before accepting the requested implementation or preferred way of working. Ask whether IT should adapt to the process or the business should adapt to standard IT; neither is automatically right. Compare only credible alternatives: custom automation, use of an existing standard process with business adaptation, controlled manual execution, and lightweight configuration or procedure. Manual work is not inherently a defect, and uncommon exceptions do not automatically warrant custom code.

Separate who receives the benefit from who funds implementation, carries support/change/security work, and loses capacity for other priorities. The requesting department may save minutes while another team acquires years of maintenance. A budget transfer is not a total saving. Equally, costs borne by IT can be justified by substantial business value: do not give either department an automatic veto or treat local cost allocation as the business case.

Use frequency, effort per transaction, expected lifetime, and error consequences to compare business execution with implementation, testing, monitoring, maintenance, training/adoption, future changes, and opportunity cost. Respect actual regulatory, customer, and operational constraints; a cheap standard process that violates them is not a credible alternative. Prefer the simplest sustainable option with adequate value and safeguards, not maximum automation or minimum build cost.

When useful, use one common time horizon: total cost = IT implementation + ongoing IT maintenance/support + business execution + operational risk + change/adoption costs. Avoid double counting and do not price risk away when a hard requirement disqualifies an option. Derive effort from supplied volumes; use supplied estimates/ranges or explicitly hypothetical sensitivity assumptions, never fabricated rates, savings, or financial precision. Name missing costs that could change the recommendation.

For meaningful decisions, conclude with a concise comparison: recommendation, main economic/operational tradeoff, who benefits and bears structural burdens, material assumptions, and reversal conditions. A small table may help; no financial model or exhaustive cost inventory is needed for a trivial decision. Existing capabilities remain options, not requirements, and economics alone does not establish architectural invariants.

For relationship integrity, prefer the simplest reliable check at the relevant boundary: an existing constraint, schema or type check, contract test, referential check, static analysis, or CI/deployment validation. Existing catalog or lineage capabilities may help when already available. Ask what catches a stale reference or changed meaning and who maintains that check. Do not introduce centralized governance, a metadata framework, or continuous monitoring unless the risk and scale justify it. Stop once important links are supported and remaining uncertainty is proportionate.
